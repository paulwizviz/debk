# Double-Entry Bookkeeping Concepts

Double-entry bookkeeping is a fundamental accounting method where every financial
transaction is recorded in at least two accounts. This system ensures that the
accounting equation always remains in balance.

## The Accounting Equation

The foundation of double-entry bookkeeping is the accounting equation:

```text
Assets = Liabilities + Equity 
```

- **Assets:** Resources owned by the business (e.g., Cash, Amount owes,
  Inventory, Equipment).
- **Liabilities:** Obligations or debts owed to outside parties (e.g., Accounts
  Payable, Loans).
- **Equity:** The owner's residual interest in the business after deducting
  liabilities (e.g., Retained Earnings, Common Stock).

The equation can be further expanded out as follows:

```text
Equity = Owner's equity - Dividends + Retained Earnings
```

Retained Earnings are profit held for future use and that is calculated as follows.

```text
Retained Earnings = Revenue - Expenses
```

## Debits and Credits

In double-entry bookkeeping, a transaction involves a flow of economic benefit from a source to a destination.

Economic benefit is the potential for an asset to contribute directly or indirectly to the flow of an entity's cash.

Transactions are recorded as debits and credits. Credit represents the source of economic benefit. Debit represents the destination of economic benefit.

We can view debit and credit by decomposing account equation this way:

```text
Asset = Liabilities + Owner's Equity - Dividends + Revenue - Expenses
```

Doing a little rearrangement we have:

```text
Dividends + Expenses + Assets = Liabilities + Owner's Equity + Revenue
```

The left hand side of the equation represents debits. The right hand side of the equation represents the credits.

Whether a debit or credit increases or decreases an account depends on the
account type.

| Account Type | Increase | Decrease | Normal Balance |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Assets | Debit | Credit | Debit |
| Liabilities | Credit | Debit | Credit |
| Equity | Credit | Debit | Credit |
| Revenue | Credit | Debit | Credit |
| Expenses | Debit | Credit | Debit |

### When to Debit or Credit

To determine whether to record a debit or a credit, follow these steps:

1. **Identify the Account Type:** Determine if the account is an Asset, Liability, Equity, Revenue, or Expense.
2. **Determine the Direction:** Is the account balance increasing or decreasing as a result of the transaction?
3. **Apply the Rule:**
   - **Debit** to:
     - Increase an **Asset** or **Expense** account.
     - Decrease a **Liability**, **Equity**, or **Revenue** account.
   - **Credit** to:
     - Increase a **Liability**, **Equity**, or **Revenue** account.
     - Decrease an **Asset** or **Expense** account.

### Definitions and Examples

- **Assets:** Resources owned by a business that have future economic value. *Example:* Cash in bank or office machinery.
- **Liabilities:** Financial obligations or debts that a business owes to external parties. *Example:* A bank loan or trade payables.
- **Equity:** The residual interest in the assets of the business after deducting all its liabilities. *Example:* Share capital or retained earnings.
- **Revenue:** Income generated by the sale of goods or services. *Example:* Sales revenue or service fees.
- **Expenses:** The costs incurred in the process of generating revenue. *Example:* Rent, utilities, or staff wages.

**Rule:** Total Debits must always equal Total Credits for every transaction.

## Core Components

### 1. Accounts

A record in the general ledger that is used to collect and store similar
transactions. Accounts are often categorised into a **Chart of Accounts (COA)**.

### 2. Journal Entry

The initial recording of a financial transaction in chronological order. Each
entry includes the date, accounts affected, and the amounts to be debited and
credited.

### 3. General Ledger (GL)

The "book of final entry" that summarises all journal entries by account. It
provides a complete record of all financial transactions over the life of the
business.

### 4. Trial Balance

 A report that lists the balances of all general ledger accounts. Its primary
purpose is to prove that the total debits equal the total credits.

### 5. Financial Statements

- **Balance Sheet:** Reports the financial position at a specific point in time
  (Assets, Liabilities, Equity).
- **Income Statement (P&L):** Reports the financial performance over a period of
  time (Revenue, Expenses).

## The Accounting Cycle

1. **Identify Transactions:** Collect receipts, invoices, and other source
   documents.
2. **Journalise:** Record transactions in the journal.
3. **Post to Ledger:** Transfer journal entries to the general ledger.
4. **Prepare Trial Balance:** Verify that debits equal credits.
5. **Adjusting Entries:** Record accruals, deferrals, and depreciation.
6. **Financial Statements:** Generate the Balance Sheet and Income Statement.
7. **Closing Entries:** Zero out temporary accounts (Revenue and Expenses) to
   Equity.

## Permanent vs. Temporary Accounts

To understand how the accounting cycle concludes, it is important to distinguish between two types of accounts:

### 1. Permanent (Real) Accounts

These accounts represent the ongoing financial position of the business and are reported on the **Balance Sheet**. Their balances **carry forward** from one accounting period to the next.

- **Includes:** Assets, Liabilities, and Equity.
- **Status:** Never zeroed out at year-end.

### 2. Temporary (Nominal) Accounts

These accounts track financial activity over a specific period and are reported on the **Income Statement**.

- **Includes:** Revenue, Expenses, and Dividends/Drawings.
- **Status:** **Closed** (reset to zero) at the end of each accounting period.

## Closing the Books

While the General Journal and Ledger are continuous records, "Closing the Books" is the process performed at the end of a year (or month) to reset temporary accounts:

1. **Transfer Balances:** The net balance of all Revenue and Expense accounts (Net Income) is moved to a permanent Equity account, such as **Retained Earnings**.
2. **Zero Out:** Once the transfer is complete, the Revenue and Expense accounts begin the new period with a balance of $0.
3. **Continuous History:** The General Journal remains a permanent, chronological record of every transaction ever made, but the *ledger balances* for income and expenses start fresh each year to measure that year's specific performance.

## Example Accounting Flow

To illustrate the flow, here is the scenario involving a hypothetical small business.

- 1 Mar 202X, the business receives initial investment of $1,000 from its founder named Alex. Alex transferred the money from his personal bank account (number: `abc-1234`) in ABC bank into the business's XYZ bank account (number: `xyz-1234`).
- 3 Apr 202X, the business withdraw $200 in cash from business bank account to be held as petty cash.
- 5 May 202X, the business use $50 of the petty cash to buy computer desk and chair.
- 7 May 202X, the business receives $4000 from a company named Acme, for service rendered. Acme transfer the money from its bank QZB account (number: `qzb-3456`) to the business XYZ bank account (number: `xyz-1234`).
- 1 Jun 202X, the business employs Alice and Bob, each at a salary of $1,000 per month (total $2,000). The first payment is made on 30 Jun 202X.
- 15 Jun 202X, the business receives a recurring $4,000 payment for services.

### 3. Post to Ledger

The amounts are transferred to the respective ledger accounts. For example, the **Cash (XYZ Bank) - 1010** ledger would show:

- **Increases (Debits):** $1,000 + $4,000 + $4,000 = $9,000.
- **Decreases (Credits):** $200 (Petty Cash) + $2,000 (Salaries) = $2,200.
- **Current Balance:** $6,800 (Debit).

### 4. Impact on Accounting Equation

After all transactions, the accounting equation remains in balance:

```text
Assets ($6,800 [1010] + $150 [1020] + $50 [1500]) = Liabilities ($0) + Equity ($1,000 [3010] + $8,000 [4010] - $2,000 [5010])
$7,000 = $7,000
```
