# Double-Entry Bookkeeping Concepts

Double-entry bookkeeping is a fundamental accounting method where every financial transaction is recorded in at least two accounts. This system ensures that the accounting equation always remains in balance.

## The Accounting Equation

The foundation of double-entry bookkeeping is the accounting equation:

```text
Assets = Liabilities + Equity 
```

- **Assets:** Resources owned by the business (e.g., cash, trade and other debtors,
  inventory, equipment).
- **Liabilities:** Obligations owed to third parties (e.g., trade creditors, loans).
- **Equity:** The residual interest in the assets after deducting liabilities
  (e.g., share capital, retained earnings). For a UK **Ltd**, this is often called
  **shareholders' funds**. Cash subscribed for **ordinary shares** is credited to
  **share capital** (and sometimes **share premium**). Unincorporated businesses may
  use **capital** or **current** accounts instead.

The equation can be further expanded out as follows:

```text
Equity = Share capital and reserves - Dividends + Retained earnings
```

Retained earnings are profit held for future use and are calculated as follows:

```text
Retained earnings = Revenue - Expenses
```

## Account Types and Definitions

To record transactions, we must first categorise the impact into five primary account types:

- **Assets:** Resources owned by a business that have future economic value. *Example:* Cash in bank or office machinery.
- **Liabilities:** Financial obligations or debts that a business owes to external parties. *Example:* A bank loan or trade payables.
- **Equity:** The residual interest in the assets of the business after deducting all its liabilities. *Example:* Share capital or retained earnings.
- **Revenue (turnover):** Income from the sale of goods or services. *Example:* sales or service fees.
- **Expenses:** The costs incurred in the process of generating revenue. *Example:* Rent, utilities, or staff wages.

## Debits and Credits

We can view debit and credit by decomposing the accounting equation this way:

```text
Asset = Liabilities + Equity - Dividends + Revenue - Expenses
```

Doing a little rearrangement we have:

```text
Dividends + Expenses + Assets = Liabilities + Equity + Revenue
```

The left-hand side of the equation represents debits. The right-hand side of the equation represents the credits.

Whether a debit or credit increases or decreases an account depends on the
account type.

| Account Type | Increase | Decrease | Normal Balance |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Assets | Debit | Credit | Debit |
| Liabilities | Credit | Debit | Credit |
| Equity | Credit | Debit | Credit |
| Revenue | Credit | Debit | Credit |
| Expenses | Debit | Credit | Debit |

### When to Debit or Credit

To determine whether to record a debit or a credit, follow these steps:

1. **Identify the Account Type:** Determine if the account is an Asset, Liability, Equity, Revenue, or Expense.
2. **Determine the Direction:** Is the account balance increasing or decreasing as a result of the transaction?
3. **Apply the Rule:**
   - **Debit** to:
     - Increase an **Asset** or **Expense** account.
     - Decrease a **Liability**, **Equity**, or **Revenue** account.
   - **Credit** to:
     - Increase a **Liability**, **Equity**, or **Revenue** account.
     - Decrease an **Asset** or **Expense** account.

**Rule:** Total Debits must always equal Total Credits for every transaction.

## Core Components

### Financial Transactions

A financial transaction is an exchange or movement of value between accounts. In a double-entry system, it represents the simultaneous recording of a source of funds (Credit) and an application of funds (Debit), ensuring the ledger remains in balance.

### Accounts

A record in the general ledger that is used to collect and store similar transactions. Accounts are often categorised into a **Chart of Accounts (COA)**.

### Journal Entry

The initial recording of a financial transaction in chronological order. Each entry includes the date, accounts affected, and the amounts to be debited and credited.

### General Ledger (GL)

The "book of final entry" that summarises all journal entries by account. It provides a complete record of all financial transactions over the life of the business.

### Trial Balance

A report that lists the balances of all general ledger accounts. Its primary purpose is to prove that the total debits equal the total credits.

### Financial Statements

- **Statement of financial position (balance sheet):** Reports the financial position at a specific point in time (Assets, Liabilities, Equity).
- **Profit and loss account (P&L):** Reports the financial performance over a period of time (Revenue, Expenses).

## The Accounting Cycle

1. **Identify Transactions:** Collect receipts, invoices, and other source documents.
2. **Record journal entries:** Post transactions to the general journal.
3. **Post to Ledger:** Transfer journal entries to the general ledger.
4. **Prepare Trial Balance:** Verify that debits equal credits.
5. **Adjusting Entries:** Record accruals, deferrals, and depreciation.
6. **Financial Statements:** Generate the balance sheet and profit and loss account.
7. **Closing entries:** Zero out temporary accounts (revenue and expenses) to equity.

## Closing the Books

While the General Journal and Ledger are continuous records, "Closing the Books" is the process performed at the end of a year (or month) to reset temporary accounts and update the business's permanent equity.

### Permanent (Real) Accounts

These accounts represent the ongoing financial position of the business and are reported on the **balance sheet**. Their balances **carry forward** from one accounting period to the next.

- **Includes:** Assets, Liabilities, and Equity.
- **Status:** Never zeroed out at year-end.

### Temporary (Nominal) Accounts

These accounts track financial activity over a specific period and are reported on the **profit and loss account**.

- **Includes:** Revenue, expenses, dividends (Ltd) or **drawings** (unincorporated).
- **Status:** **Closed** (reset to zero) at the end of each accounting period.

### The Closing Process

1. **Transfer balances:** The net balance of all revenue and expense accounts (profit for the period) is moved to a permanent equity account, such as **retained earnings**.
2. **Zero out:** Once the transfer is complete, the revenue and expense accounts begin the new period with a balance of £0.
3. **Continuous History:** The General Journal remains a permanent, chronological record of every transaction ever made, but the *ledger balances* for income and expenses start fresh each year to measure that year's specific performance.
