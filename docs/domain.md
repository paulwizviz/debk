# Double-Entry Bookkeeping Concepts

Double-entry bookkeeping is a fundamental accounting method where every financial transaction is recorded in at least two accounts. This system ensures that the accounting equation always remains in balance.

## The Accounting Equation

The foundation of double-entry bookkeeping is the accounting equation:

```text
Assets = Liabilities + Equity 
```

- **Assets:** Resources owned by the business (e.g., Cash, Amount owed,
  Inventory, Equipment).
- **Liabilities:** Obligations or debts owed to third parties (e.g., Trade
  Payables, Loans).
- **Equity:** The owner's residual interest in the business after deducting
  liabilities (e.g., Retained Earnings, Share Capital).

The equation can be further expanded out as follows:

```text
Equity = Owner's equity - Dividends + Retained Earnings
```

Retained Earnings are profit held for future use and are calculated as follows:

```text
Retained Earnings = Revenue - Expenses
```

## Account Types and Definitions

To record transactions, we must first categorise the impact into five primary account types:

- **Assets:** Resources owned by a business that have future economic value. *Example:* Cash in bank or office machinery.
- **Liabilities:** Financial obligations or debts that a business owes to external parties. *Example:* A bank loan or trade payables.
- **Equity:** The residual interest in the assets of the business after deducting all its liabilities. *Example:* Share capital or retained earnings.
- **Revenue:** Income generated by the sale of goods or services. *Example:* Sales revenue or service fees.
- **Expenses:** The costs incurred in the process of generating revenue. *Example:* Rent, utilities, or staff wages.

## Debits and Credits

We can view debit and credit by decomposing the accounting equation this way:

```text
Asset = Liabilities + Owner's Equity - Dividends + Revenue - Expenses
```

Doing a little rearrangement we have:

```text
Dividends + Expenses + Assets = Liabilities + Owner's Equity + Revenue
```

The left-hand side of the equation represents debits. The right-hand side of the equation represents the credits.

Whether a debit or credit increases or decreases an account depends on the
account type.

| Account Type | Increase | Decrease | Normal Balance |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Assets | Debit | Credit | Debit |
| Liabilities | Credit | Debit | Credit |
| Equity | Credit | Debit | Credit |
| Revenue | Credit | Debit | Credit |
| Expenses | Debit | Credit | Debit |

### When to Debit or Credit

To determine whether to record a debit or a credit, follow these steps:

1. **Identify the Account Type:** Determine if the account is an Asset, Liability, Equity, Revenue, or Expense.
2. **Determine the Direction:** Is the account balance increasing or decreasing as a result of the transaction?
3. **Apply the Rule:**
   - **Debit** to:
     - Increase an **Asset** or **Expense** account.
     - Decrease a **Liability**, **Equity**, or **Revenue** account.
   - **Credit** to:
     - Increase a **Liability**, **Equity**, or **Revenue** account.
     - Decrease an **Asset** or **Expense** account.

**Rule:** Total Debits must always equal Total Credits for every transaction.

## Core Components

### 1. Financial Transactions

A financial transaction is an exchange or movement of value between accounts. In a double-entry system, it represents the simultaneous recording of a source of funds (Credit) and an application of funds (Debit), ensuring the ledger remains in balance.

### 2. Accounts

A record in the general ledger that is used to collect and store similar transactions. Accounts are often categorised into a **Chart of Accounts (COA)**.

### 3. Journal Entry

The initial recording of a financial transaction in chronological order. Each entry includes the date, accounts affected, and the amounts to be debited and credited.

### 4. General Ledger (GL)

The "book of final entry" that summarises all journal entries by account. It provides a complete record of all financial transactions over the life of the business.

### 5. Trial Balance

A report that lists the balances of all general ledger accounts. Its primary purpose is to prove that the total debits equal the total credits.

### 6. Financial Statements

- **Balance Sheet:** Reports the financial position at a specific point in time (Assets, Liabilities, Equity).
- **Profit and Loss Statement (P&L):** Reports the financial performance over a period of time (Revenue, Expenses).

## The Accounting Cycle

1. **Identify Transactions:** Collect receipts, invoices, and other source documents.
2. **Journalise:** Record transactions in the journal.
3. **Post to Ledger:** Transfer journal entries to the general ledger.
4. **Prepare Trial Balance:** Verify that debits equal credits.
5. **Adjusting Entries:** Record accruals, deferrals, and depreciation.
6. **Financial Statements:** Generate the Balance Sheet and Profit and Loss Statement.
7. **Closing Entries:** Zero out temporary accounts (Revenue and Expenses) to Equity.

## Closing the Books

While the General Journal and Ledger are continuous records, "Closing the Books" is the process performed at the end of a year (or month) to reset temporary accounts and update the business's permanent equity.

### 1. Permanent (Real) Accounts

These accounts represent the ongoing financial position of the business and are reported on the **Balance Sheet**. Their balances **carry forward** from one accounting period to the next.

- **Includes:** Assets, Liabilities, and Equity.
- **Status:** Never zeroed out at year-end.

### 2. Temporary (Nominal) Accounts

These accounts track financial activity over a specific period and are reported on the **Profit and Loss Statement**.

- **Includes:** Revenue, Expenses, and Dividends/Drawings.
- **Status:** **Closed** (reset to zero) at the end of each accounting period.

### 3. The Closing Process

1. **Transfer Balances:** The net balance of all Revenue and Expense accounts (Net Income) is moved to a permanent Equity account, such as **Retained Earnings**.
2. **Zero Out:** Once the transfer is complete, the Revenue and Expense accounts begin the new period with a balance of $0.
3. **Continuous History:** The General Journal remains a permanent, chronological record of every transaction ever made, but the *ledger balances* for income and expenses start fresh each year to measure that year's specific performance.

## Proposed Software Solution

Alice, as the owner of ACME Private Limited, intends to employ a digital bookkeeping solution to maintain the financial integrity of her enterprise. Her logical expectations of the system are focused on how it manages the lifecycle of business value and enforces accounting rigor:

### 1. System Initialisation and Identity

Alice expects a secure, personal environment where her business records are isolated and protected. This begins with establishing her identity and defining the unique **Chart of Accounts (COA)** that reflects her business model—identifying the specific Assets, Liabilities, Equity, Revenue, and Expense accounts (such as EFG Bank, Bank Loan, and Office Equipment) required to track ACME's operations.

### 2. Logical Event Capture (The ACME Workflow)

Alice expects to record business events as they occur, with the system ensuring that every "application of funds" is matched by a "source of funds." Her primary use cases include:

- **Capital and Funding Capture:** Capturing the inception of the business through initial equity investments and long-term debt (e.g. the $40,000 EFG Bank loan).
- **Asset Lifecycle Management:** Recording the acquisition of physical resources and the non-cash adjusting entries, such as monthly depreciation, to ensure the "Net Book Value" on her balance sheet reflects reality.
- **Operational Complexity (Payroll):** Handling multi-line transactions where a single event must be split—such as recording gross salary while simultaneously accounting for net payments and tax liabilities.
- **Revenue Recognition:** Capturing income from services rendered (e.g. IniTech) to measure performance.
- **Internal Treasury Movements:** Tracking the movement of value within the business (e.g. bank-to-petty-cash transfers) without mischaracterising them as profit or loss.

### 3. Integrity and Automated Validation

Alice expects the system to be "balanced by design." It must logically enforce the double-entry rule at the point of entry, preventing any transaction where total debits do not equal total credits from being recorded. This ensures the fundamental accounting equation ($Assets = Liabilities + Equity$) is never violated.

### 4. On-Demand Financial Visibility

Alice expects the system to provide immediate, real-time insights into the state of her business. She needs the ability to derive structured reports from the cumulative journal of entries:

- **Transaction History (The Journal):** A chronological and logical audit trail of every event.
- **Performance Measurement (Profit and Loss):** A report showing the net income derived from revenues and expenses over a specific period.
- **Financial Position (Balance Sheet):** A snapshot of the company's total assets, liabilities, and residual equity at any given moment.

### 5. Standalone Local Execution

Alice intends to use this solution as a private, standalone tool. The system is explicitly designed for local execution only, with no requirement for cloud-based infrastructure or remote connectivity. All financial data, including the Chart of Accounts and transaction logs, will be persisted within her local environment to ensure absolute control and privacy over ACME's sensitive financial information. There is no direct connectivity to third-party applications like bank accounts or customers' applications. Alice will collect data from all sources and enter manually.

The application system architecture is monolithic. The web page, web server and computational logic are packaged as a single executable as per Go embedding architecture.
